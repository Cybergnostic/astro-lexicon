## Terms first encountered or formally established in Part I

### Astrology
Abū Maʿshar treats astrology as a systematic, teachable science rather than guesswork. In Chapter 2 he defines it through knowledge of planetary and celestial natures, the properties of their indications, and the effects associated with celestial motions in the world beneath the Moon.

### Science of the Stars
A broader conceptual category containing two species in Chapter 2: the “science of the universe” and astrology.

### Science of the Universe
The astronomical branch: spheres, their quantity and quality, motions, order, distance, inclination, planetary speeds, eclipses, occultations, and related measurements. This is not silently renamed “astronomy” in the database because the historical wording and conceptual division are important.

### Indication
A foundational technical concept. Celestial conditions can indicate terrestrial conditions and events. Abū Maʿshar also speaks separately of celestial powers and actions, so “indication” must not be reduced to physical causation.

### Celestial Power
Part I Chapter 3 develops the causal side of Abū Maʿshar’s framework. Celestial bodies act through their motions on sublunary bodies that are naturally receptive to them. This is kept distinct from the doctrine of indication.

### Fifth Nature
The celestial spheres and stars are assigned a fifth nature, distinct from the four mutable sublunary elements. The term is philosophical-cosmological but structurally important for Abū Maʿshar’s explanation of celestial action.

### Sublunary World
The mutable terrestrial realm under the sphere of the Moon, where generation, corruption, increase, decrease, mixture, and elemental change occur.

### Four Elements / Natures
Fire, air, water, and earth. The English translation frequently uses “natures” while glossing them as elements. Their mixture and alteration form the material framework for Abū Maʿshar’s account of celestial action.

### Coming-to-be and Passing-away
The paired processes of generation and corruption in the sublunary world. Part I repeatedly links them to elemental alteration under celestial motion.

### Form / Natural Composition / Natured Things
Chapter 4 introduces an explicit natural-philosophical vocabulary. Forms organize elemental material into species; natural composition is the organization of an individual; “natured things” are the generated animals, plants, and minerals produced from the four elements. These are retained because later astrological doctrine presupposes this framework.

### Universal Indication / Particular Indication
Chapter 5 distinguishes general indications concerning genera, species, elements, and classes from indications applying to particular individuals, bodily parts, qualities, conditions, and degree-specific matters. This distinction is important enough to preserve explicitly in the ontology.

### Possibility / Contingency
Chapter 5 contains a long Aristotelian defence of the possible against strict necessity. Deliberation and choice operate in the field of things that can occur or not occur before actualization. Abū Maʿshar then argues that astrology concerns such future possibilities and their eventual outcomes.

### Foreknowledge
The central concept of Chapter 6. Abū Maʿshar argues that advance knowledge can allow complete avoidance of some harms, partial mitigation of others, preparation for temporary hardships, arrangement of affairs before unavoidable events, and preparation for future good.

### Revolution of the World-Year
Named in Parts I §§5.26 and 6.7 as a framework for general or mundane indications affecting populations, cities, or regions. Detailed rules are deferred until later material or other works.

### Revolution of the Year of a Nativity
Named as an annual predictive framework for a particular native. In Chapter 6 it appears alongside the nativity itself and a question as a source of judgment about future conditions.

### Astrological Question
Part I explicitly names a question concerning a person’s condition as one of the astrological contexts from which a particular future matter can be judged. Technical interrogational rules are not yet given here.

### Lunar Mansions
Twenty-eight lunar stations. Chapter 2 cites experienced peoples using the Moon’s presence in these mansions to infer weather and other changes.

### Waxing / Waning
The increase and decrease of lunar light appear as distinct lunar conditions in observational practice. Kept as separate but paired concepts.

### Easternness / Westernness
Mentioned in Chapter 2 among planetary conditions tracked by experienced practitioners. Their precise solar-phase definitions are intentionally deferred until the later dedicated planetary-condition sections are reached.

## Terms first encountered or formally established in Part II

### Fixed Stars / Stellar Magnitude / Constellation
Part II Chapter 1 distinguishes the seven planets from 1,022 slow-moving fixed stars, classifies the latter in six brightness magnitudes, and organizes them into forty-eight constellations. Abū Maʿshar explicitly says the constellation figures are formed in imagination and thought.

### Zodiac / Sign
Chapter 2 gives the central zodiacal belt as 360 degrees divided into twelve equal signs of thirty degrees each. The source also discusses the twelve stellar constellations on the Sun’s path, so the database must distinguish equal zodiacal signs from pictorial constellations rather than silently treating them as identical.

### Universal and Particular Indication
Part II sharpens the distinction already encountered in Part I. The twelve zodiacal constellations/signs are assigned universal indication, while the other constellations are assigned more particular indications.

### Triplicity
The twelve signs are grouped by elemental nature into four groups of three: fiery, earthy, airy, and watery. Abū Maʿshar sometimes uses the same translated word “triplicity” for the signs belonging to such a group, a translation issue that must remain visible in editorial notes.

### Tropical / Fixed / Bicorporeal Signs
Part II Chapters 6–7 derive the three sign modalities from the seasonal cycle. Tropical signs begin a seasonal quadrant and mark change; fixed signs establish its nature; bicorporeal signs mix the departing season with the approaching one. Preserve “tropical” and “bicorporeal” as source terminology rather than automatically replacing them with modern “cardinal” and “mutable”.

### Quadrant of the Sphere
The annual zodiacal cycle is divided into four seasonal quadrants. Their beginning, middle, and end provide the conceptual basis for the twelve signs and the three modalities.

### Masculine / Feminine Signs
The natural classification alternates masculine and feminine beginning with Aries masculine and Taurus feminine. Abū Maʿshar distinguishes this from an accidental house-based alternation counted from the ascendant and gives priority to the natural order when the two disagree.

### Diurnal / Nocturnal Signs
Part II Chapter 9 gives another alternating classification beginning Aries diurnal, Taurus nocturnal. This is a classification of signs and must not be collapsed into planetary sect.

## Terms first encountered or formally established in Part III

### Seven Planets / Swift-moving Stars
Part III Chapter 1 explains why the seven planets are given broad, general indication. Their motions and conditions change more rapidly and more variously than those of the fixed stars, so Abū Maʿshar associates them with faster-changing general conditions and beginnings of actions.

### Astrologer
Chapter 2 gives a formal definition: the astrologer knows the conditions and indications of the stars and reports what they signify for a specified present or future time.

### Obvious Indication / Hidden or Subtle Indication
Chapter 2 explicitly limits the practical art. Some indications and their signified matters are sufficiently evident to know and use; others are too subtle, remote, or difficult for the astrologer to comprehend exactly. Abū Maʿshar does not deny that the latter exist, but says they are not necessary for practical judgment.

### Luminaries
The Sun and Moon are treated together as the two luminaries. Their indications are said to be especially evident because of the Sun’s size and the Moon’s proximity, and because both act through rays as well as motion.

### Ebb and Flow of the Tide
Chapters 4–8 form a long historical natural-astrological treatment of tides. The Moon is assigned the principal action producing the flow, while the return of the water in ebb is attributed to the water’s own nature. Local geography, salinity and density, winds, lunar phase, lunar distance and latitude, solar season, and other factors modify the result. This is preserved as historical doctrine, not presented as modern science.

### Critical Days
Chapter 9 connects the Moon’s daily motion and specified angular relationships to its earlier position with the “known” or critical days used in judging the changing condition of a patient.

## Terms first encountered or formally established in Part IV

### Planetary Nature
Part IV is explicit that hot, cold, wet, and dry are not literal elemental constituents of celestial bodies. They are attributed to planets from the actions observed in the sublunary world. This is a critical distinction for interpreting every later use of planetary “nature”.

### Planetary Property
Abū Maʿshar distinguishes a planet’s property from its attributed elemental nature. Property explains why the same planet can produce different kinds of fortune and misfortune according to condition and receptivity.

### Benefic / Malefic / Mixed
Beneficence is associated with tempering, agreement, generation, life, and preservation; maleficence with excess, discord, corruption, and destruction. Mercury is especially mixed and readily takes on the qualities of associated signs and planets.

### In the Nature of the Benefics / Malefics
Part IV Chapter 6 does not simply reclassify a malefic as benefic or vice versa. A malefic under favourable conditions can act “in the nature of the benefics”; a benefic under corrupting conditions can act “in the nature of the malefics”. The original class remains conceptually distinct from the temporary manifestation.

### Ascending / Descending in the Orb of Apogee
Part IV Chapter 7 uses ascent and descent through the apogee orb as major modifiers of a planet’s attributed qualities. The effect is planet-specific and combines with sign, quadrant, term, and solar relation.

### Direct Motion / Station / Retrogradation
Stations and changes between direct and retrograde motion enter the qualitative phase doctrine. For the superior planets the sequence from eastern appearance through stations and opposition is assigned successive qualitative states; Mercury and Venus have their own phase sequence.

### Planetary Masculinity / Femininity
The planetary classification is grounded primarily in heat/activity versus wetness/passivity, but manifestation varies with eastern/western phase and quadrant. It must remain separate from the masculine/feminine classification of signs.

### Diurnal / Nocturnal Planets
Saturn, Jupiter, and the Sun are diurnal; Mars, Venus, and the Moon are nocturnal. Mercury is conditional. Abū Maʿshar explains this through temperateness by day or night and then modifies the indications through easternness and westernness. This belongs to sect doctrine, not to the separate Part II classification of diurnal/nocturnal signs.

### Head / Tail of the Dragon
The Head of the lunar ǧawzahar is associated with ascent, leadership, and a measure of good fortune; the Tail with descent and misfortune. Neither is absolutely fixed in manifestation: each can under some conditions produce effects resembling the opposite class.

## Editorial observations from Parts I–IV

1. Part I is largely foundational and philosophical rather than a list of technical astrological rules. It nevertheless supplies the conceptual grammar of the rest of the work.
2. “Indication” and “celestial power” are related but not interchangeable. One concerns what celestial conditions signify; the other concerns the causal or natural action Abū Maʿshar attributes to celestial motion.
3. “Nature” is heavily overloaded in the translation. It can mean one of the four elements, a qualitative constitution, a planetary attribution, or a natural property. Do not collapse these uses.
4. Abū Maʿshar explicitly argues from universal to particular indications. This gives us a useful ontology distinction for later entries: some statements concern classes, others individuals or degree-specific particulars.
5. His treatment of possibility and choice is nuanced. He rejects simple fatalism, while also saying that when an event is actualized it enters necessity or impossibility. Do not summarize his position as either modern free will or strict determinism.
6. Chapter 6 of Part I defines the value of astrology primarily through foreknowledge and preparation rather than through an assumption that every indicated event can be prevented.
7. “Revolution of the world-year”, “revolution of the year of a nativity”, and “question” appear before their methods are developed. They are recorded now and should be refined later rather than overdefined from Part I alone.
8. Part II confirms that source terminology should be preserved even when later English astrology uses different labels: `tropical` rather than automatically `cardinal`, and `bicorporeal` rather than automatically `mutable`.
9. Part II requires a strict distinction between zodiacal signs and stellar constellations.
10. Diurnal/nocturnal signs are not the same object as planetary sect.
11. Part III makes an important epistemological distinction: astrology is not presented as exhaustive knowledge of every possible stellar indication. Abū Maʿshar explicitly recognizes indications that are too subtle or difficult for practical human judgment.
12. The long tidal chapters contain many physical and geographical details, but these should not each become separate lexicon entries. Their value is mainly as evidence for Abū Maʿshar’s model of lunar action and modifying factors.
13. Medical and natural claims in Part III are historical source material and must not be presented on the public site as modern medical or physical science.
14. Part IV makes the nature/property distinction essential. “Saturn is cold and dry” in this source should not be read as saying Saturn is literally made of elemental coldness and dryness.
15. Benefic and malefic are stable classes in the discourse, but their effects are not mechanically fixed. Conditions, placement, phase, and planetary mixtures can produce benefic-like or malefic-like manifestations.
16. “In the nature of the benefics/malefics” should remain its own relational concept rather than being flattened into “is benefic/is malefic”.
17. Planetary masculinity/femininity and diurnal/nocturnal planets are separate ontological objects from masculine/feminine signs and diurnal/nocturnal signs.
18. The source edition is copyrighted. Database summaries are original paraphrases; no long translated passages are stored.
