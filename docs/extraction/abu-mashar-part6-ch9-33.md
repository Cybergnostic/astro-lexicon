# Abū Maʿshar — Part VI, Chapters 9–33

Sequentially reviewed from *The Great Introduction to Astrology*, Brill 2019, PDF pp. 650–727 / original pp. 636–713.

## Status

Part VI is now completely reviewed. Chapters 1–8 are documented in the main Abū Maʿshar ledger; this file records Chapters 9–33.

## Chapter groups

### Chapter 9 — countries and regions
A large geographical correspondence catalogue assigns countries, regions, landscapes and types of places to the signs. These are preserved as historical geographical astrology, not modern geography.

### Chapters 10–11 — motion/rest and voiced signs
Chapter 10 gives a mobile/resting classification dependent on agreement between signs and their rulers. It is not the same classification as tropical/fixed/bicorporeal signs.

Chapter 11 introduces voiced signs and ties them to classes or conditions of people. Chapter 18 later refines the voice doctrine into powerful, moderate, weak and voiceless sign classes.

### Chapter 12 — body correspondence
The twelve signs are assigned bodily regions from head downward to feet, with historical medical correspondences attached to them. This is recorded as medieval medical astrology only and should never be presented as modern medical guidance.

### Chapters 13–21 — proper human indications
These chapters catalogue sign indications for wealth patterns, appearance, fertility, barrenness, voice, bodily conditions and other human topics. Not every phrase becomes a standalone lexicon term. Only recurring technical classifications are promoted.

Chapter 16 is especially useful for the later vocabulary of fecund and barren signs. It distinguishes signs having many children, twin-bearing signs, few-child signs and barren signs.

### Chapters 22–25 — natural correspondences
Chapter 22 assigns birds, quadrupeds, beasts of prey, vermin and aquatic animals to signs.

Chapter 23 gives trees, plants, seeds and grasses.

Chapter 24 distinguishes sign correspondences to rain, flowing water, river water, stagnant water, and things produced by fire.

Chapter 25 assigns the four triplicities to the four directions and distributes the signs within each direction among the cardinal and intermediate winds.

### Chapter 26 — cardines, quadrants and twelve places
This is one of the most important chapters in the whole work for house doctrine.

Four cardines divide the sphere into quadrants. Each quadrant is divided into three places: a cardine, a succedent place, and a place cadent from the cardine. The resulting twelve places are numbered from the ascendant.

Abū Maʿshar gives topical significations for all twelve places, then spends substantial space explaining why those meanings are assigned. He presents a planetary-sphere-order rationale, geometrical rationales based on the place's condition relative to cardines and oppositions, and also reports another view according to which every place simply has its own intrinsic property.

This means later house meanings should not be flattened into a single unexplained keyword list: the source itself distinguishes several mechanisms by which a place acquires significance.

### Planetary joys
Chapter 26 finally gives a sufficient definition of joy for a full lexicon entry. A planet rejoices in a place whose indications correspond to its own indications:

- Mercury — first place
- Moon — third
- Venus — fifth
- Mars — sixth
- Sun — ninth
- Jupiter — eleventh
- Saturn — twelfth

This resolves the earlier Part V deferral where joy was merely named as a planetary share without enough explanation.

### Chapters 27–30 — quadrant doctrines
Chapter 27 classifies quadrants as spirit without body, neither spirit nor body, body without spirit, and body with spirit.

Chapter 28 explains the four-element mixture of the cardines.

Chapter 29 assigns colours to quadrants and places.

Chapter 30 distinguishes ascending/descending and long/short portions of the sphere.

These are separate historical layers on top of the twelve-place framework and should not be treated as definitions of the places themselves.

### Chapter 31 — fourfold natural correspondence
The four humoral natures are correlated with directions, winds, seasons, groups of three signs, quadrants, quarters of day/night and ages of life. This is a major example of Abū Maʿshar's analogical fourfold cosmology.

### Chapters 32–33 — time divisions, planetary hours and weekday rulers
Chapter 32 derives a fourfold division of day and night by analogy with the four seasons, and from each quarter's three conditions derives twelve hours for day and twelve for night.

Chapter 33 assigns planetary hours in repeating sphere order. Starting from the first hour of Sunday: Sun, Venus, Mercury, Moon, Saturn, Jupiter, Mars, then repeat. The planet reached at the twenty-fifth count rules the next weekday.

The resulting weekday rulers are Sun, Moon, Mars, Mercury, Jupiter, Venus and Saturn from Sunday through Saturday.

## Editorial observations

1. Part VI contains huge catalogues of correspondences. The lexicon should index recurring doctrines, not create a separate term for every animal, country, body part or descriptive adjective.
2. Mobile/resting signs are distinct from the modality doctrine of Part II.
3. Voiced/voiceless signs are a historical sign-quality doctrine and should remain source-specific.
4. Fecund/barren classifications are historical astrological doctrines, not biological claims.
5. The twelve-place chapter demonstrates that source-specific house meanings must be preserved. Later authors may add, remove or reorganize meanings.
6. `Cardine`, `succedent`, and `cadent` are structural terms and deserve independent entries.
7. `Joy` can now be promoted from a deferred Part V mention to a properly defined house doctrine.
8. Planetary joys depend on correspondence between planetary and place indications, not merely a memorized place-number table.
9. Historical medical and bodily material must be clearly labelled as historical doctrine rather than modern health information.
10. Part VI is now fully sequentially reviewed.
