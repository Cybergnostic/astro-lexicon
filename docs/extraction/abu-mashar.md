## Terms first encountered or formally established in Part I

### Astrology
Abū Maʿshar treats astrology as a systematic, teachable science rather than guesswork. In Chapter 2 he defines it through knowledge of planetary and celestial natures, the properties of their indications, and the effects associated with celestial motions in the world beneath the Moon.

### Science of the Stars
A broader conceptual category containing two species in Chapter 2: the “science of the universe” and astrology.

### Science of the Universe
The astronomical branch: spheres, their quantity and quality, motions, order, distance, inclination, planetary speeds, eclipses, occultations, and related measurements. This is not silently renamed “astronomy” in the database because the historical wording and conceptual division are important.

### Indication
A foundational technical concept. Celestial conditions can indicate terrestrial conditions and events. Abū Maʿshar also speaks separately of celestial powers and actions, so “indication” must not be reduced to physical causation.

### Celestial Power
Part I Chapter 3 develops the causal side of Abū Maʿshar’s framework. Celestial bodies act through their motions on sublunary bodies that are naturally receptive to them. This is kept distinct from the doctrine of indication.

### Fifth Nature
The celestial spheres and stars are assigned a fifth nature, distinct from the four mutable sublunary elements. The term is philosophical-cosmological but structurally important for Abū Maʿshar’s explanation of celestial action.

### Sublunary World
The mutable terrestrial realm under the sphere of the Moon, where generation, corruption, increase, decrease, mixture, and elemental change occur.

### Four Elements / Natures
Fire, air, water, and earth. The English translation frequently uses “natures” while glossing them as elements. Their mixture and alteration form the material framework for Abū Maʿshar’s account of celestial action.

### Coming-to-be and Passing-away
The paired processes of generation and corruption in the sublunary world. Part I repeatedly links them to elemental alteration under celestial motion.

### Form / Natural Composition / Natured Things
Chapter 4 introduces an explicit natural-philosophical vocabulary. Forms organize elemental material into species; natural composition is the organization of an individual; “natured things” are the generated animals, plants, and minerals produced from the four elements. These are retained because later astrological doctrine presupposes this framework.

### Universal Indication / Particular Indication
Chapter 5 distinguishes general indications concerning genera, species, elements, and classes from indications applying to particular individuals, bodily parts, qualities, conditions, and degree-specific matters. This distinction is important enough to preserve explicitly in the ontology.

### Possibility / Contingency
Chapter 5 contains a long Aristotelian defence of the possible against strict necessity. Deliberation and choice operate in the field of things that can occur or not occur before actualization. Abū Maʿshar then argues that astrology concerns such future possibilities and their eventual outcomes.

### Foreknowledge
The central concept of Chapter 6. Abū Maʿshar argues that advance knowledge can allow complete avoidance of some harms, partial mitigation of others, preparation for temporary hardships, arrangement of affairs before unavoidable events, and preparation for future good.

### Revolution of the World-Year
Named in Parts I §§5.26 and 6.7 as a framework for general or mundane indications affecting populations, cities, or regions. Detailed rules are deferred until later material or other works.

### Revolution of the Year of a Nativity
Named as an annual predictive framework for a particular native. In Chapter 6 it appears alongside the nativity itself and a question as a source of judgment about future conditions.

### Astrological Question
Part I explicitly names a question concerning a person’s condition as one of the astrological contexts from which a particular future matter can be judged. Technical interrogational rules are not yet given here.

### Lunar Mansions
Twenty-eight lunar stations. Chapter 2 cites experienced peoples using the Moon’s presence in these mansions to infer weather and other changes.

### Waxing / Waning
The increase and decrease of lunar light appear as distinct lunar conditions in observational practice. Kept as separate but paired concepts.

### Easternness / Westernness
Mentioned in Chapter 2 among planetary conditions tracked by experienced practitioners. Their precise solar-phase definitions are intentionally deferred until the later dedicated planetary-condition sections are reached.

## Editorial observations from Part I

1. Part I is largely foundational and philosophical rather than a list of technical astrological rules. It nevertheless supplies the conceptual grammar of the rest of the work.
2. “Indication” and “celestial power” are related but not interchangeable. One concerns what celestial conditions signify; the other concerns the causal or natural action Abū Maʿshar attributes to celestial motion.
3. “Nature” is heavily overloaded in the translation. It can mean one of the four elements, a qualitative constitution, or a natural property. Avoid a single generic `nature` entry.
4. Abū Maʿshar explicitly argues from universal to particular indications. This gives us a useful ontology distinction for later entries: some statements concern classes, others individuals or degree-specific particulars.
5. His treatment of possibility and choice is nuanced. He rejects simple fatalism, while also saying that when an event is actualized it enters necessity or impossibility. Do not summarize his position as either modern free will or strict determinism.
6. Chapter 6 defines the value of astrology primarily through foreknowledge and preparation rather than through an assumption that every indicated event can be prevented.
7. “Revolution of the world-year”, “revolution of the year of a nativity”, and “question” appear before their methods are developed. They are recorded now and should be refined later rather than overdefined from Part I alone.
8. The source edition is copyrighted. Database summaries are original paraphrases; no long translated passages are stored.
